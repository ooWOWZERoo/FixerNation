# Idea Lab (grades 9-12): four situations, for your approval

Written 2026-11-01. Outline only: no step text is written until you approve or edit each line. This would be the second direct game for the Creative Innovator attribute (the first is Fix-It Workshop, grades 3-5), and the first for grades 9 to 12. Each run would play one of four stories.

**Links (draft, yours to sign off):** Portrait of a Graduate: Creative Innovator (direct), Effective Communicator (supporting), Critical Thinker (supporting), Global Citizen (supporting). NY SEL 3B Decision-making (supporting), 3C Acting for school and community (supporting).

**The idea:** a student has a real problem to solve with a limit (time, money, or people), and several workable ways to solve it. The game walks through how to come up with, shape and test an original idea. There is no single best idea: each of the three ideas at the idea step is workable, with its own strength and cost, and the feedback says what that idea is good at and what it risks.

**How each story plays (existing five-step story engine, no new code):** the five steps become an idea cycle:

| Step (engine key) | In this game |
|---|---|
| Recognize | **Find the real need:** who needs this, and how do you find out? |
| Impact | **See what exists:** what has been tried, and what is missing? |
| Consider | **Three directions:** a safe improvement, a bolder original idea, or a mix (all workable) |
| Choose | **Test it small:** how do you try the idea, share credit and use feedback? |
| Reflect | **Private reflection** (never saved or sent) |

Steps 1 and 2 give three options (one careful, two believable but incomplete); step 3 gives three ideas that are all marked "also good" (so no idea is called best, the same rule as Fix-It Workshop); step 4 gives four (careful, also good, unsafe, ask a trusted adult). The writing rules are the ones a test enforces for the other story games: balanced option lengths (within 1.4 times), no dashes, fictional names, and a believable "unsafe" option. In this game "unsafe" means a move that hurts the idea or other people: copying without credit, launching without permission, or ignoring feedback.

**Limit to say plainly:** a multiple-choice game can compare ideas and teach the process and the trade-offs. It cannot judge a student's own original idea. A free-text idea step would be a separate, privacy-reviewed decision.

## The four stories

### 1. A peer tutoring exchange
**Situation:** students struggle in different subjects and many do not ask for help. You want to set up a way for students to help each other. You have three weeks and no budget.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Ask a wide group of students what holds them back from asking for help | "Assume that students are too proud"; "Ask only students who already tutor" |
| Impact | Find out what the school already offers, and what students say is missing | "Skip the research, because tutoring is obviously good"; "Copy another school's program exactly" |
| Consider (3 ideas) | A sign-up board for 20-minute help sessions at lunch (simple, fast, limited reach); a student-made short video library for common questions (original, takes time to make); a swap system where each student lists one subject they can help with and one they need (bolder, needs a coordinator) | |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** try one version with one grade for two weeks, count sessions and ask users what to change. **Also good:** ask two teachers to review a draft before you launch. **Unsafe:** launch school-wide without asking anyone and use classmates' grades to match them. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask a teacher or counselor to help set it up safely. | |
| Reflect | "What is one idea of yours that got better because someone gave you honest feedback?" | |

### 2. Less waste at school snack sales
**Situation:** the snack sales after school leave a pile of single-use wrappers and cups. You want an idea that cuts the waste without ending the sales. The student council has $100 and two months.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Watch a few sales, count what is thrown away, and ask students and the cafeteria staff what they think | "Assume that students do not care about waste"; "Guess at what is thrown away" |
| Impact | Find out what the sale earns and what alternatives would cost, so that the idea can work in practice | "Skip the costs, because it is for a good cause"; "Ban single-use items without a plan" |
| Consider (3 ideas) | A "bring your own cup" discount (cheap, relies on habits); reusable cups with a small deposit (more lasting, needs washing); a switch to compostable items (easy for students, costs more) | |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** test one idea at two sales, count the waste again, and share the results with the council. **Also good:** ask the club that runs the sales to try it for a month. **Unsafe:** change the sale without asking and tell students that it is required. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask a teacher or custodian about what the school allows. | |
| Reflect | "What small change in your own habits would make a difference to somebody else?" | |

### 3. Getting announcements to everyone
**Situation:** students miss school announcements because they are read aloud in a noisy hall, and some students cannot hear or read them easily. You want a way for announcements to reach everyone.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Ask students who miss announcements why, including students who use captions or read more slowly | "Assume that students do not pay attention"; "Ask only students who already follow the news" |
| Impact | Find out how announcements are made now and which formats are easy to add | "Replace everything with an app"; "Ignore the current way, since it is out of date" |
| Consider (3 ideas) | A weekly one-page summary posted in homerooms and online (simple, reaches many); a short daily video with captions (more engaging, takes more work); a text or email digest students opt into (reaches phones, needs permission) | |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** try one format for two weeks, ask a mix of students if it helped, and adjust. **Also good:** ask the office to review the draft before the first one goes out. **Unsafe:** send messages from a school list without approval. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask the office or a teacher about the school's communication rules. | |
| Reflect | "How do you decide whether an idea is good for people who are different from you?" | |

### 4. A low-cost fundraiser for a community garden
**Situation:** your class wants to raise $200 in three weeks for a school garden. The first idea in the group is a bake sale, but another class already did one last month.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Ask who will give and what they would value, not only what you would enjoy making | "Assume that people will buy anything for a good cause"; "Repeat the bake sale since it worked for others" |
| Impact | Look at what other fundraisers did at the school, what they raised and what they cost | "Ignore past events"; "Guess that a new event will raise more" |
| Consider (3 ideas) | A "plant swap and sale" with cuttings from students' homes (original, low cost, depends on donations); a garden-themed game night with a small entry fee (fun, needs planning and an adult); a "sponsor a seedling" drive where families fund one plant at a time (simple, slower) | |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** pilot one idea on a small scale, track the money, and give credit to everyone who helped. **Also good:** ask last month's class for tips on what worked. **Unsafe:** use another group's idea and name without asking or giving credit. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask the advisor or principal about the rules for collecting money. | |
| Reflect | "When you build on someone else's idea, how do you want to give credit?" | |

## Spread checks

- **Four different kinds of problem:** a service, a physical product, an access fix and an event. None repeats a Fix-It Workshop or Community Voice problem.
- **Each story has three different kinds of idea:** a simple one, an original one and a mix, with real strengths and costs. None is called best.
- **The careful move differs by story:** pilot small and measure, test and share results, try one format and adjust, and pilot and give credit. "Ask a trusted adult" is the fourth option, never the first.
- **The unsafe options are about the idea going wrong:** launching without asking, copying without credit, skipping approval, using private information.
- **Reading level:** about grade 8 to 12, plain and concrete.
