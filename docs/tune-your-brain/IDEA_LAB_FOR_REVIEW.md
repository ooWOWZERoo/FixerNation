# Idea Lab (grades 9-12): the four finished stories, for your read

Written 2026-11-01 from the outline you approved. **Held: not committed or deployed until you have read it.**

**What the tests enforce (the same rules as the other story games):** steps 1 to 3 give three options and step 4 gives four (careful, also good, unsafe, ask a trusted adult); feedback on every option; option lengths within 1.4 times; the careful answer is the longest in at most 2 of the 4 steps; no dashes; fictional names; a private reflection that is never saved or sent. The idea step (step 3) is the exception, as in Fix-It Workshop: it has three workable directions, all marked "also good", and each one's feedback names a strength and a cost, so no idea is called best.

**Please check:** story 3 (students who use captions or read more slowly), the "unsafe" options (launching without asking, copying without credit, using private information), and the feedback lines students read. A multiple-choice game teaches the process and the trade-offs; it cannot judge a student's own idea.

## Story 1: A peer tutoring exchange

**1. Recognize (find the real need).** Students at your school struggle in different subjects, and many do not ask for help. You want to set up a way for students to help each other, with three weeks and no budget. What do you do first?

- (careful move) Ask a wide group of students what holds them back from asking for help.
  - *Hearing the real reasons keeps you from solving the wrong problem, and a wide group shows what most people need.*
- (believable but incomplete) Assume that students are too proud to ask, and design something that pushes them to do it.
  - *Pride may be part of it, but it is a guess. If the real reason is something else, the idea will miss.*
- (believable but incomplete) Ask only the students who already tutor, since they know the most about helping.
  - *They know about helping, but not about why others do not ask. The people who do not ask hold the answer.*

**2. Impact (see what exists).** What do you need to know before you design anything?

- (careful move) What the school already offers, and what students say is still missing.
  - *Knowing what exists keeps you from repeating it, and what is missing shows where an idea would matter.*
- (believable but incomplete) Nothing more, since tutoring is obviously good and the idea does not need any research.
  - *Tutoring is good, but your version needs to fit your school. Quick research saves weeks of work.*
- (believable but incomplete) How another school ran its program, so that you can copy it exactly as it was.
  - *Another school's program can inspire you, but copying it exactly ignores what your students need.*

**3. Consider (three directions, all workable).** You have three directions in mind. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) A sign-up board for 20-minute help sessions at lunch, which is simple and fast to start.
  - *This is quick to launch and easy to understand. It reaches only some students, and it depends on people showing up at lunch.*
- (also good) A short video library made by students that answers the most common questions.
  - *This is original and always available. It takes weeks to make, and it only helps with questions that you have thought of.*
- (also good) A swap board where each student lists one subject to help with and one that they need.
  - *This is bolder and shows that everyone has something to offer. It needs a coordinator, and it takes time to match people.*

**4. Choose (test small).** What is the best next step?

- (careful move) Try one version with one grade for two weeks, count the sessions, and ask users what to change.
  - *A small trial shows what works before you spend more time, and users tell you what you missed.*
- (also good) Ask two teachers to review a draft before you launch, and then adjust it.
  - *Also good. Teachers can spot problems early, though you still need real users to try it.*
- (unsafe) Launch school-wide without asking anyone, and use classmates' grades to match students.
  - *Using grades without permission breaks privacy, and launching without asking can end the project before it starts.*
- (ask a trusted adult) Ask a teacher or counselor to help you set it up safely and fairly for every student.
  - *An adult can help with privacy and fairness, and they may know of students who would help.*

**5. Private reflection.** What is one idea of yours that got better because someone gave you honest feedback?


## Story 2: Less waste at school snack sales

**1. Recognize (find the real need).** The after-school snack sales leave a pile of single-use wrappers and cups. You want an idea that cuts the waste without ending the sales. The student council has $100 and two months. What do you do first?

- (careful move) Watch a few sales, count what is thrown away, and ask students and staff what they think.
  - *Counting and asking give you real facts, and the people who run the sales know what is practical.*
- (believable but incomplete) Assume that students do not care about waste, and plan a campaign to make them care.
  - *That is a guess, and it may be unfair. Many students may care but have no easy alternative.*
- (believable but incomplete) Guess at what is thrown away, since it is probably mostly wrappers and cups.
  - *Your guess may be right, but a count is much more convincing. It also shows where to aim.*

**2. Impact (see what exists).** What do you need to know before you design anything?

- (careful move) What the sales earn and what alternatives would cost, so that the idea can work in practice.
  - *An idea that cuts waste but loses the sales will not last, so you need both numbers.*
- (believable but incomplete) Nothing about costs, because it is for a good cause and the money will sort itself out in time.
  - *A good cause helps, but a $100 budget is a real limit. Ignoring cost can sink a good idea.*
- (believable but incomplete) Whether you can ban single-use items outright, since that would end the waste at once.
  - *A ban is simple, but it might end the sales or upset people. A plan that people want to follow works better.*

**3. Consider (three directions, all workable).** You have three directions in mind. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) A "bring your own cup" discount, which costs little and relies on students building the habit.
  - *This is cheap and easy to start. It only works if students remember their cups, and it may not change habits fast.*
- (also good) Reusable cups with a small deposit, which lasts longer but needs a way to wash them.
  - *This cuts waste for a long time. It needs space and people to wash the cups, and it costs more to start.*
- (also good) A switch to compostable cups and wrappers, which is easy for students but costs more.
  - *Students do not have to change anything, so more people take part. It costs more each sale and needs a place to compost.*

**4. Choose (test small).** What is the best next step?

- (careful move) Test one idea at two sales, count the waste again, and share the results.
  - *Before-and-after counts show whether it worked, and sharing them helps the council decide what to do next.*
- (also good) Ask the club that runs the sales to try it for a month, and ask for their feedback.
  - *Also good. The club knows the sales best, and their buy-in makes it more likely to last.*
- (unsafe) Change the sale without asking anyone, and tell students that it is now required.
  - *Skipping the people who run the sales and making it a rule can cause anger. Your idea may be dropped.*
- (ask a trusted adult) Ask a teacher or the custodian what the school allows before you test anything.
  - *Rules about food and waste are real, and a teacher or custodian can tell you what is possible.*

**5. Private reflection.** What small change in your own habits would make a difference to someone else?


## Story 3: Getting announcements to everyone

**1. Recognize (find the real need).** Students miss school announcements because they are read aloud in a noisy hall, and some students cannot hear or read them easily. You want a way for announcements to reach everyone. What do you do first?

- (careful move) Ask students who miss announcements why, including students who use captions or read more slowly.
  - *The people who miss them can tell you what is in the way, and different students will name different problems.*
- (believable but incomplete) Assume that students are not paying attention, and plan a way to get their attention.
  - *Attention may be part of it, but it is a guess. If the problem is access, a louder message will not help.*
- (believable but incomplete) Ask only the students who already follow the news, since they know what is important.
  - *They are easy to ask, but they are not the ones who miss things. You need the others.*

**2. Impact (see what exists).** What do you need to know before you design anything?

- (careful move) How announcements are made now, and which formats would be easy to add.
  - *Knowing the current process and its limits shows where a small change would help the most.*
- (believable but incomplete) Nothing, because an app would fix everything and the old way is out of date anyway.
  - *An app might help, but it can leave out students without phones. Check what already works first.*
- (believable but incomplete) How to replace the current way entirely, since a new system is better than a patch.
  - *A full replacement is a big job. A change that fits into what the school already does is easier to adopt.*

**3. Consider (three directions, all workable).** You have three directions in mind. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) A weekly one-page summary posted in homerooms and online, which is simple and reaches many.
  - *This is easy to make and read. It only comes out weekly, so urgent news may not fit.*
- (also good) A short daily video with captions, which is more engaging but takes more work each day.
  - *A video grabs attention and works for many learners. It takes someone to make it every day, and it needs captions that are checked.*
- (also good) A text or email digest that students opt into, which reaches phones but needs permission.
  - *This reaches students where they are. It needs a sign-up and school permission, and it leaves out students without phones.*

**4. Choose (test small).** What is the best next step?

- (careful move) Try one format for two weeks, ask a mix of students if it helped, and adjust.
  - *A short trial with different students shows what works for whom, and you can change it before it spreads.*
- (also good) Ask the office to review the first draft before it goes out, and then fix anything they flag.
  - *Also good. The office knows what must be included, and they can help it reach the right people.*
- (unsafe) Send the messages from a school list without approval, so that it starts right away.
  - *Using a school list without permission can break rules and get your project shut down.*
- (ask a trusted adult) Ask the office or a teacher about the school's communication rules before you start anything.
  - *Asking first shows that you respect the rules, and the office may help you do it properly.*

**5. Private reflection.** How do you decide whether an idea is good for people who are different from you?


## Story 4: A low-cost fundraiser for a garden

**1. Recognize (find the real need).** Your class wants to raise $200 in three weeks for a school garden. The first idea in the group is a bake sale, but another class already held one last month. What do you do first?

- (careful move) Ask who might give and what they would value, not only what you would enjoy making.
  - *Starting with what givers value shows you what would actually bring in money.*
- (believable but incomplete) Assume that people will buy anything for a good cause, so any event will work.
  - *A good cause helps, but people still choose. An event that people want will raise much more.*
- (believable but incomplete) Repeat the bake sale, since it worked for others and it is easy to run.
  - *It is easy, but another class just did one, so buyers may be tired of it.*

**2. Impact (see what exists).** What do you need to know before you design anything?

- (careful move) What past fundraisers at the school raised and cost, so that you can aim higher.
  - *Past results show what is realistic, and costs show how much of the money you will actually keep.*
- (believable but incomplete) Nothing about past events, because yours will be different and better than theirs.
  - *It may be, but past results tell you what to expect. Ignoring them is guessing.*
- (believable but incomplete) Whether a new event would raise more, because new things always bring in more money.
  - *New can help, but it can also fail. You need a reason, not only a hope.*

**3. Consider (three directions, all workable).** You have three directions in mind. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) A plant swap and sale with cuttings from students' homes, which is original and low cost.
  - *This is unusual and costs little. It depends on donations, so how much you raise is hard to predict.*
- (also good) A garden-themed game night with a small entry fee, which is fun but needs planning.
  - *A game night draws families and can raise a lot. It needs an adult to run it and a date that works for everyone.*
- (also good) A "sponsor a seedling" drive, where families fund one plant at a time, which is simple but slow.
  - *This is easy to explain and run. It only grows slowly, so reaching $200 in three weeks is a stretch.*

**4. Choose (test small).** What is the best next step?

- (careful move) Pilot one idea on a small scale, track the money, and give credit to everyone who helped.
  - *A small test shows what works before you commit, and giving credit keeps helpers wanting to come back.*
- (also good) Ask last month's class for tips on what worked and what did not.
  - *Also good. They learned things that you do not know yet, and it avoids repeating their mistakes.*
- (unsafe) Use another group's idea and name without asking, and take the credit for it yourself.
  - *Copying without asking or giving credit is unfair to them, and it could damage your own reputation.*
- (ask a trusted adult) Ask your advisor or the principal about the rules for collecting money at school.
  - *Rules about money are strict, and asking first prevents trouble later.*

**5. Private reflection.** When you build on someone else's idea, how do you want to give credit?
