- [Plausible but incomplete] Mia is just being lazy and does not care about the project.
  - Feedback: Maybe, but that is a guess. It is worth finding out what is really going on first.

**2. Impact.** If Mia's part is not finished, what happens?

- [Best] The whole team's project could be incomplete, and Mia might feel embarrassed or stressed too.
  - Feedback: Right. This affects the whole team, including Mia.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Only Mia will get in trouble, so it is not really the team's problem.
  - Feedback: A team project usually means a team result. It is everyone's problem, not just Mia's.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways your team could handle this?

- [Best] Ask Mia if she needs help, and figure out a plan together.
  - Feedback: Having a real plan, made together, beats guessing what to do.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Just finish her part without telling her.
  - Feedback: This solves the deadline, but it skips Mia entirely and does not find out what she actually needs.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Ask Mia privately if everything is okay and offer to help with her part.
  - Feedback: A private check-in respects Mia and gets you real information before anyone decides anything.
- [Also good] Suggest to the whole team that you divide up Mia's remaining work fairly, with her input.
  - Feedback: Also a solid move. Fair redivision, with Mia included, keeps this from feeling like a punishment.
- [Not safe] Tell the teacher Mia is ruining the project without talking to her first.
  - Feedback: Going straight to blame, without ever asking Mia what is going on, could embarrass her for something that might not even be her fault.
- [Ask for help] If Mia seems really overwhelmed or upset, let the teacher know so an adult can help find a plan.
  - Feedback: Asking a trusted adult for help is a reasonable choice, especially if something bigger than a school project seems to be going on.

**5. Reflect.** What is one way you could check in with a teammate who seems stuck, next time it happens?

### Story 2: The borrowed markers

**1. Recognize.** You borrowed Dev's brand-new marker set to finish a poster. When you open the case at home, one marker has been left uncapped and it has dried out.

- [Best] Notice that you feel worried, and that Dev will probably find the dried marker.
  - Feedback: Good noticing. Worry is a clue that this matters to you, and facing it early gives you more choices.
- [Plausible but incomplete] It is only one marker out of many in a big set, so it probably will not matter to Dev.
  - Feedback: It might be small, but the set is new and it is Dev's. How big a mistake feels depends on whose thing it is.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Maybe the marker was already dry when Dev lent you the set, so it is not your problem.
  - Feedback: That could be true, but you do not know it. Guessing that the blame belongs somewhere else can keep you from checking.

**2. Impact.** If you say nothing and return the set, what is likely to happen?

- [Best] Dev may find it later and wonder what happened, and lending you things could get harder.
  - Feedback: Yes. Trust grows when people are honest about small things, and it gets harder to rebuild after a surprise.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Nothing much, since Dev has other markers and probably will not check every one in the case.
  - Feedback: He might not check. But you would be carrying the secret, and the next time he looks, it could be a bigger deal.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Dev will be upset for a day, but things will go back to normal once he forgets about it.
  - Feedback: He may forget. It is more likely he would remember that you knew and said nothing.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could handle this?

- [Best] Tell Dev what happened, say sorry, and ask what he would like you to do about it.
  - Feedback: Telling him first lets Dev feel respected, and it gives the two of you a chance to fix it together.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Buy a new marker and slip it into the case so Dev never has to know.
  - Feedback: Replacing it is kind, but doing it in secret skips the honest part. Dev still does not get to hear it from you.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Return the set, say it was great to use, and let Dev notice the marker on his own.
  - Feedback: This sounds friendly, but it leaves Dev to find the problem alone, and it may feel like you hid it.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive thing to do right now?

- [Best] Tell Dev about the dried marker when you return the set, and say sorry.
  - Feedback: You are honest, you take responsibility, and Dev hears it from you. That is hard to do and it builds trust.
- [Also good] Offer to replace the marker, and ask Dev if he wants the same color.
  - Feedback: Also a good move. Offering to make it right, and asking what Dev wants, treats him as the owner of the decision.
- [Not safe] Return the set quietly and hope he never opens that particular marker.
  - Feedback: Hoping nobody notices a mistake rarely works, and if Dev finds it first, the silence looks worse than the dried marker.
- [Ask for help] Ask a parent or a teacher how to start the conversation with Dev about it.
  - Feedback: Asking a trusted adult for help finding the words is a good idea, especially when you feel nervous about it.

**5. Reflect.** What is one thing you could say to someone when you have made a mistake with something that is not yours?

### Story 3: The rules that keep changing

**1. Recognize.** At recess you are playing tag. Ravi keeps changing the rules so that he is never "it". A couple of friends are getting annoyed, and one has started to walk away.

- [Best] Notice that the game feels unfair to several people at once, and not only to one of them.
  - Feedback: Good noticing. When more than one person feels the same way, it is worth taking seriously.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Ravi is just very competitive, so this is how he always plays and there is nothing to do.
  - Feedback: Some people play hard to win. A rule that changes for only one person is a different thing, though.
- [Plausible but incomplete] It is only a game, so the best thing is to stop thinking about it and play something else.
  - Feedback: Leaving is one choice. It leaves the problem for the friends who stay and for Ravi, who may not see it.

**2. Impact.** If the rules keep changing, what is likely to happen to the game?

- [Best] More friends may quit, the game may fall apart, and Ravi may never learn why people are upset.
  - Feedback: Yes. Both the game and the friendships are affected, and Ravi cannot fix what he does not know about.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Ravi will notice sooner or later and fix it himself, so nobody needs to do anything yet.
  - Feedback: He might. People often do not see their own habits unless someone tells them kindly.
- [Plausible but incomplete] The annoyed friends will get over it, because tag is over in a few minutes anyway.
  - Feedback: The feeling may fade, but it can come back the next time, and this happens at recess most days.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could handle this?

- [Best] Suggest that the group agrees on the rules before the next round starts.
  - Feedback: Agreeing on rules ahead of time takes the blame off one person and helps every game after this one.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Tell everyone loudly that Ravi is cheating so that he has to stop right now.
  - Feedback: It would be noticed, but calling someone out in front of others often makes them defend themselves instead of listen.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Quietly start a different game with the friends who are annoyed and leave Ravi out.
  - Feedback: That fixes today's game, but Ravi is left out without knowing why, and the problem may come back with someone else.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Say, "Can we pick the rules together before we start again?"
  - Feedback: A clear request that includes everyone gives Ravi a way to say yes without feeling attacked.
- [Also good] Talk to Ravi alone later and tell him how the rule changes felt.
  - Feedback: A private talk can work well, since Ravi will not feel embarrassed. It is slower, and the game may be over by then.
- [Not safe] Tell the group that Ravi is a cheater and nobody should play with him.
  - Feedback: Labeling Ravi turns a rule problem into a problem about who he is, and a label like that can follow him around.
- [Ask for help] Ask the recess helper to help the group agree on fair rules.
  - Feedback: A helper can keep things calm and fair when a group cannot settle it alone. That is a good use of an adult.

**5. Reflect.** What is one fair way to decide the rules before a game starts?

### Story 4: A secret that is not yours

**1. Recognize.** Your friend Lena tells you she is nervous about reading aloud tomorrow. At lunch, another classmate asks you, "Is Lena scared about the reading?"

- [Best] Notice that Lena told you this in trust, and that the question asks you to pass it on.
  - Feedback: Good noticing. Spotting that something was shared in trust is the first step to keeping it that way.
- [Plausible but incomplete] It is only a feeling about reading, so it is not really a secret and sharing it would be fine.
  - Feedback: Lena did not say it was a secret, but she chose you to tell. Feelings like nerves are personal.
- [Plausible but incomplete] The classmate probably just wants to help, so it is nice to tell them what you know.
  - Feedback: They might mean well. Even so, how Lena feels is hers to share, and you would be answering for her.

**2. Impact.** If Lena finds out you told, what is likely to happen?

- [Best] Lena may feel embarrassed, stop telling you things, and feel more nervous about reading tomorrow.
  - Feedback: Yes. Trust is hard to rebuild, and sharing her feeling could make tomorrow harder for her.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Lena will probably not mind, since lots of people feel nervous about reading aloud.
  - Feedback: It is common to feel nervous, but that does not mean she wants other people to know.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Lena may be upset for a bit, but she will understand that you were only trying to help.
  - Feedback: She might forgive you. She might also wonder what else you will share, and good intentions do not undo the hurt.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could answer the classmate?

- [Best] Say, "That is Lena's to share," and then change the subject kindly.
  - Feedback: It is honest and kind. You are not hiding anything, and you are not speaking for Lena.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Say, "I don't know," even though you do, so the classmate stops asking.
  - Feedback: It ends the question, but it is not true. Being honest and protecting a friend can go together.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Say a little, like "She is a bit worried," so it feels like you are being honest.
  - Feedback: A little still counts as telling. Once part of it is out, the rest is easy to guess.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Tell the classmate it is Lena's to share, then ask Lena if she wants help practicing.
  - Feedback: You protect her privacy, you stay honest, and you offer help. That is what a trusted friend does.
- [Also good] Tell the classmate you are not sure, then let Lena know someone was asking about her.
  - Feedback: This keeps her feelings private and lets Lena decide what to do. Saying "That is Lena's to share" would be more honest than "I'm not sure."
- [Not safe] Tell the classmate that Lena is nervous, since it is probably not a big deal anyway.
  - Feedback: Deciding it is not a big deal is making a choice for Lena. She trusted you to keep it.
- [Ask for help] Ask a teacher for ideas on how to support Lena without sharing her feelings with anyone.
  - Feedback: A teacher can help you support Lena in ways that keep her feelings private. Asking is a smart move.

**5. Reflect.** What helps you know whether something is yours to share?

  - Feedback: That might work sometimes, but it does not give you a real plan if the feeling sticks around or happens again.

  - Feedback: This manages your own reaction and still opens the door for your friends to understand your side.
- [Ask for help] If the feeling does not go away, talk to a trusted adult, like a teacher or family member, about how to handle it.

### Story 2: The missed catch

**1. Recognize.** In kickball, your team loses by one run after you miss an easy catch. A few teammates groan, and your face feels hot.

- [Best] Notice that you feel embarrassed, and maybe a bit angry too, and that this is a normal reaction.
  - Feedback: Naming the feeling is the first step. It helps your thinking stay clear while the hot feeling passes.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Tell yourself that you do not care at all, because it is only a game and nobody will remember.
  - Feedback: Saying you do not care can feel safer, but the feeling is still there, and it tends to show up some other way.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Decide the miss was not your fault, because the sun was in your eyes and the ball was fast.
  - Feedback: Maybe the sun did make it harder. Still, deciding it was all outside you makes it hard to learn from it.

**2. Impact.** If the feeling takes over, what is likely to happen for the rest of recess?

- [Best] You may snap at a teammate or walk off upset, and people may remember that more than the miss.
  - Feedback: Yes. A strong feeling can make a small moment bigger than it needs to be.
- [Plausible but incomplete] You will feel bad for a moment, then forget about it, so nothing about the rest of recess changes.
  - Feedback: Sometimes it fades fast. Feelings that are ignored often come back at a worse moment.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Your teammates will stay annoyed with you, and there is nothing you can do to change that.
  - Feedback: How teammates react depends a lot on what you do next, so you have more say than it feels like.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could handle the feeling?

- [Best] Take a few slow breaths and remember that one missed catch does not define you.
  - Feedback: Calming your body first helps you choose what to do next instead of reacting.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Stay quiet for the rest of recess so nobody has a chance to say anything more about it.
  - Feedback: Staying quiet may feel safe, but it leaves the hot feeling with you and the team without a word from you.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Joke about it loudly so everyone laughs and the groaning moment is quickly forgotten.
  - Feedback: A joke can ease things for some people. If it covers up how you feel, the feeling may still be waiting afterward.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Take a breath, then say to the team, "I missed that one, and I will get the next."
  - Feedback: Owning the miss calmly shows your team you can handle mistakes, and it usually ends the groaning.
- [Also good] Step aside for a minute to calm down, then rejoin the game when you feel ready.
  - Feedback: A short break is a good tool, and rejoining shows you are ready to keep going.
- [Not safe] Say the ball was bad and the sun was too bright, so it was not your fault.
  - Feedback: Excuses may protect you for a moment, but they can make teammates feel you are not taking the game seriously.
- [Ask for help] Tell the recess helper you are feeling upset and ask for a minute to calm down.
  - Feedback: Telling a helper is a strong move, and you can do it whenever a feeling feels too big.

**5. Reflect.** What can you tell yourself the next time you make a mistake in front of other people?

### Story 3: The spelling test

**1. Recognize.** You studied all week, but your spelling test score is lower than you hoped. The friend next to you got every word right.

- [Best] Notice that you feel disappointed, and maybe a bit jealous too, and that both are normal.
  - Feedback: Naming both feelings is a good start. You can feel disappointed and still be happy for a friend.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Decide that you are just bad at spelling, because all that studying did not help.
  - Feedback: That is a lot of weight to put on one test. One score tells you about this test, and not much else.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Decide that spelling tests do not matter, so there is no reason to feel anything about it.
  - Feedback: It is a quick way to feel better, but you did study, which shows this mattered to you.

**2. Impact.** If you decide you are just bad at spelling, what is likely to happen?

- [Best] You may study less next week, feel worse about spelling, and miss the chance to get better.
  - Feedback: Yes. What we believe about ourselves changes how hard we try.
- [Plausible but incomplete] You will feel better right away, and that will make it easier to focus on other subjects.
  - Feedback: It may feel better for a moment, but giving up on a skill usually makes it harder later.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Your teacher will notice and give you extra help without you needing to ask for it.
  - Feedback: Teachers do notice, but they cannot always tell what a student needs. Asking is much more reliable.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could handle this?

- [Best] Look at which words you missed and pick a few to practice again tonight.
  - Feedback: A small, clear plan turns a disappointing score into something you can act on.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Ask your friend to quiz you every day until you score as high as they did.
  - Feedback: Practicing with a friend can be fun and helpful. Trying to match their score can turn it into a race that adds pressure.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Study twice as long next week, with no breaks, so that the same thing never happens again.
  - Feedback: Working harder may help, but a plan with no breaks is hard to keep up. A smaller, steady goal works better.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Pick three missed words to practice tonight and check how it goes on Friday.
  - Feedback: A small goal that you can check is how people actually get better at things.
- [Also good] Ask your teacher for ideas on how to study spelling in a different way.
  - Feedback: Teachers know many ways to study, and asking shows you care about improving.
- [Not safe] Decide that spelling is not for you and stop trying on the next test.
  - Feedback: Stopping can feel like a relief, but it closes the door on getting better, and a spelling score is only one test.
- [Ask for help] Tell your friend congratulations and ask how they like to study.
  - Feedback: Asking a friend how they study can give you ideas, and it turns a jealous feeling into something useful.

**5. Reflect.** What is one small goal you could set after a result you did not expect?

### Story 4: The part in the play

**1. Recognize.** The class play parts are announced. Your friend Zoe gets the lead role you wanted, and you get a small part.

- [Best] Notice that you feel let down and a little jealous, and also a bit happy for Zoe.
  - Feedback: Mixed feelings are normal. You can be disappointed and still care about your friend.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Tell yourself that it is not fair, and that the teacher picked Zoe for the wrong reasons.
  - Feedback: It is easy to feel that way. You do not know the teacher's reasons, though, and blaming them can keep you stuck.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Tell yourself that you are fine and that it does not bother you at all.
  - Feedback: Saying you are fine can feel safer, but a feeling you do not look at tends to pop out later.

**2. Impact.** If you keep the feeling inside, what might happen?

- [Best] You may pull away from Zoe, enjoy rehearsals less, and miss the fun of being in the play.
  - Feedback: Yes. Feelings you hide can grow into distance between friends.
- [Plausible but incomplete] It will probably fade on its own by the first rehearsal, so nothing else needs to happen.
  - Feedback: It might. If it does not, you may have already pulled away without meaning to.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Zoe will notice you are upset and fix it by offering you a bigger part or a favor.
  - Feedback: Zoe cannot change the parts, and expecting her to fix it puts a lot on her. Telling her how you feel is fairer.

**3. Consider.** What are some ways you could handle this?

- [Best] Say congratulations to Zoe, and then put your effort into making your own part great.
  - Feedback: You protect the friendship and give yourself something good to work on.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Tell Zoe you are upset, and ask her to turn down the lead so that you can have it.
  - Feedback: Being honest about feelings is good, but asking Zoe to give up her part is not fair to her.
- [Plausible but incomplete] Keep your distance from Zoe at rehearsals until your feelings settle down.
  - Feedback: A little space can help for a day. A long distance can turn a hard feeling into a lasting gap.

**4. Choose.** What is the most constructive next step right now?

- [Best] Tell Zoe congratulations, and do your best with your own part in the play.
  - Feedback: It shows kindness and gives you a clear job. Friends often remember who cheered for them.
- [Also good] Tell a trusted friend how you feel first, then talk to Zoe when you feel calmer.
  - Feedback: Talking it through first can help you say what you mean calmly. Just make sure you do get back to Zoe.
- [Not safe] Tell others that Zoe only got the lead because she is the teacher's favorite.
  - Feedback: Spreading that idea could hurt Zoe and make rehearsals tense for everyone, including you.
- [Ask for help] Ask the teacher what you can work on to earn a bigger part next time.
  - Feedback: This turns a disappointment into a plan. Teachers usually like being asked.

**5. Reflect.** What can you do with a feeling like jealousy so that it does not hurt a friendship?
