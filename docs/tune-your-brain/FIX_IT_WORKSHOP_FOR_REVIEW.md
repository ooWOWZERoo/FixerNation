# Fix-It Workshop (grades 3-5): the four finished problems, for your read

Written 2026-11-01 from the outline you approved. **Held: not committed or deployed until you have read it.** Each run plays one of the four problems. The idea step (step 3) has no single best answer: all three ideas are workable and each one's feedback names its strength and its cost. Step 5 is a private reflection that is never saved or sent.

**What the tests enforce:** three options in steps 1 to 3 and four in step 4 (one responsible, one also defensible, one unsafe, one ask an adult); step 3 is three "also good" ideas, each naming a cost or limit; option lengths within 1.4 times; the best answer is the longest in at most 2 of 3 steps; feedback on every option; no dashes; fictional names.

**Please check:** the tone toward children who are new or left out (problems 2 and 4), the "unsafe" option in each problem, and the feedback lines, which are what the child reads after each choice.

## Problem 1

**1. Listen.** At lunch, the line to pay and get your tray is so long that you only have ten minutes left to eat. Other kids are rushing too, and you want to help fix it. What do you do first?

- (careful design move) Watch the line for a few days, time how long it takes, and ask kids and the lunch staff what they notice.
  - *Timing the line and asking people gives you real facts. The lunch staff see things you cannot.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Ask only your best friends what they think, since they will probably agree with your ideas.
  - *Your friends' ideas matter, but they see the line from the same spot you do. Other kids and the lunch staff may notice something different.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Decide that kids are just too slow, and plan to tell everyone to hurry up next week.
  - *That is a guess, and it blames kids before you know the cause. Looking first usually leads to a better answer.*

**2. Name the real problem.** After a week of timing, you see that the line moves slowly at the start of lunch, when everyone arrives at once. Only one register is open. What is the real problem?

- (careful design move) The line is slow at the start because everyone arrives together and only one register is open.
  - *That is what is really happening, so your ideas can aim right at it.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Kids take too long choosing their food, so the real problem is how they behave at lunch time every day.
  - *You timed it, and the register was what slowed things down. Blaming kids would not fix that.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) The cafeteria is just too small, so there is nothing anyone can do about the line.
  - *The room may be small, but you found a part you can change. Giving up too early skips the best ideas.*

**3. Ideas (all three are workable).** You think of three ideas for fixing the line. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) Open a second register for the first ten minutes of lunch, so two lines move at once.
  - *This works fast because more people can pay at once. It needs a staff member for those ten minutes, so someone would have to agree.*
- (also good) Send classes to lunch in two groups, five minutes apart, so fewer kids arrive at the same moment.
  - *This costs no money and fixes the crowd at its start. It changes the schedule, so teachers would need to agree.*
- (also good) Add a grab-and-go cart with packaged lunches and snacks that kids can pay for very quickly.
  - *This helps kids who want something fast. It does not help everyone, because only some foods fit on a cart.*

**4. Try a small test.** Before you change lunch for the whole school, what is the best next step?

- (careful design move) Try your idea for one week with one grade, then time the line again and ask kids and staff what they think.
  - *A small test shows whether the idea works before you change things for everyone, and it lets you fix problems early.*
- (also good) Draw your plan on paper and show it to two kids and one lunch worker before you try anything.
  - *A paper test can catch mistakes cheaply. You will still need a real trial to know for sure.*
- (leaves people out or breaks a rule) Tell your class to follow your new plan on Monday without asking anyone, because you are sure it will work out well.
  - *This skips the people who run lunch, and your plan could cause new problems. Even a good idea needs permission and a test.*
- (ask the adult who can say yes) Share your idea with the principal or the lunch manager, who can say yes and help set up a trial.
  - *Asking the adults who can approve a change is smart, because they can make a real trial happen.*

**5. Private reflection.** Think about the people who felt the long line. What did you learn from listening to them that surprised you?


## Problem 2

**1. Listen.** At recess, the corner of the playground by the fence is always empty, while the big games are loud and crowded. You wonder why nobody goes to the corner. What do you do first?

- (careful design move) Watch recess for two days, and ask a few kids what they do instead of going to the corner.
  - *Watching and asking shows you what is really happening on the playground.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Decide the kids are lazy, and plan to tell them to use the whole playground.
  - *That blames kids before you know the reason. They may have a good reason for staying away from the corner.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Ask only the kids who are already in the big games, since they are the easiest to find.
  - *They are easy to find, but they are not the kids who skip the corner. You need to hear from the ones who stay away.*

**2. Name the real problem.** You learn that the corner has no shade and nothing to do. Some kids who do not like loud games stay inside at recess. What is the real problem?

- (careful design move) The corner has no shade and nothing to do, so kids who want something calm have nowhere to go.
  - *That is the real gap, so your ideas can fill it.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Kids today just do not like quiet places, so the corner is not worth the trouble of fixing.
  - *Some kids you talked to said they wanted something calm. The corner is missing something, not the kids.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) The loud games are the problem, so the best fix is to stop the games that make noise.
  - *Many kids love those games. Taking them away would help some kids and hurt others.*

**3. Ideas (all three are workable).** You think of three ideas for the corner. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) Set up a chalk and drawing station with a small table, so kids can make something calm.
  - *This gives kids a quiet activity right away. It needs chalk and a dry day, so it would not work in the rain.*
- (also good) Start a reading nook with donated books and a few mats for kids who want to sit quietly.
  - *This is peaceful and lasts a long time. It needs books and mats, plus a dry place to keep them.*
- (also good) Bring out a calm game box with cards and puzzles that a teacher keeps and shares with kids.
  - *This is easy to store and has something for everyone. It needs a grown-up to look after it.*

**4. Try a small test.** Before you change the corner for everyone, what is the best next step?

- (careful design move) Open the corner for one week, count who uses it, and ask them what they would change.
  - *A short trial shows whether kids really use it, and their answers tell you what to fix next.*
- (also good) Try just one thing first, like the chalk station on dry days, and add more if kids like it.
  - *Starting small costs less, and you learn which ideas kids like best.*
- (leaves people out or breaks a rule) Move the benches and mats to the corner yourself during recess, without asking any adult.
  - *Moving school things on your own could cause a mess or an accident, and it skips the people in charge.*
- (ask the adult who can say yes) Ask a recess helper or your teacher if you can set it up, and who could keep an eye on the corner.
  - *Asking an adult is a good move. They can say yes and find supplies, and they can keep an eye on everyone.*

**5. Private reflection.** Think about a time you wanted a calm place and could not find one. What did you do, and what would have helped?


## Problem 3

**1. Listen.** The lost-and-found box by the office is overflowing with coats and lunch boxes, and kids keep losing new things. You want to fix it. What do you do first?

- (careful design move) Look at what is in the box, ask a few classes what they have lost, and ask the custodian how often it gets emptied.
  - *Each person tells you a different piece of the story. Together they show what is really going on.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Say that kids are careless, and plan to remind everyone to keep track of their things.
  - *Reminders may help a little, but you have not found out why things stay in the box. Looking first leads to better ideas.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Ask only the kids in your own class, since they are the easiest ones for you to reach.
  - *Your class can help, but the box holds things from the whole school. Other classes may know things yours does not.*

**2. Name the real problem.** You find that most things sit in the box all year, because nobody knows what is in there. What is the real problem?

- (careful design move) Nobody can see what is in the box, so kids do not know that their things are there.
  - *If kids cannot see their things, they cannot get them back. Now your ideas can aim at that.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Kids lose too many things, so the real problem is that they do not care about their stuff.
  - *Kids told you they wanted their things back. The trouble is finding them, not caring.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) The box is too small, so a bigger box would fix everything and make the problem go away.
  - *A bigger box would hold more lost things, but it would not help kids find them.*

**3. Ideas (all three are workable).** You think of three ideas for helping kids find their things. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) Take photos of the items and make a photo board in the hallway, so kids can spot their things.
  - *This lets everyone see what is lost, all week. It takes time to take the photos and keep the board up to date.*
- (also good) Set up a Friday table at dismissal where kids can look through the lost things for ten minutes.
  - *This is simple and gives kids a real chance to look. It only works on Fridays, so things could wait all week.*
- (also good) Hold a name-label day with stickers, so more things find their way back to their owners.
  - *This helps stop the problem before it starts. It will not help with the things already in the box.*

**4. Try a small test.** Before you change how the whole school does this, what is the best next step?

- (careful design move) Try the photo board for two weeks, count how many things get claimed, and ask kids if it helped.
  - *Counting shows whether the idea really works, and the kids' answers tell you what to improve.*
- (also good) Try the Friday table for three weeks first, then compare how many things get picked up each time.
  - *A few weeks of counting gives you real numbers, and you can switch ideas if it does not work.*
- (leaves people out or breaks a rule) Throw away everything that looks old so the box looks cleaner, and then start fresh.
  - *Those things belong to someone, and some kids may still be looking. Never throw away other people's things without asking.*
- (ask the adult who can say yes) Ask the office staff and the custodian if you can put a board on the wall, and who could help take photos.
  - *Asking the adults who run the office is wise, because they can approve a spot and help make it work.*

**5. Private reflection.** Think about something you lost and got back. How did it feel, and how could the school make that happen more often?


## Problem 4

**1. Listen.** A new student in your class, Ana, got lost twice on her first day because the hallways all look alike. You want to make the school easier for new kids. What do you do first?

- (careful design move) Ask two students who joined this year what was hard and what helped, and listen to their answers.
  - *Kids who joined recently remember what it felt like, and they can tell you what helped.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Ask only the kids who have been here since kindergarten, since they know the school best.
  - *They know the building well, but they may have forgotten what it was like to be new. New kids can tell you more.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Decide that new kids should just ask for help, and plan to tell them so on their first day.
  - *Asking is hard when everything is new and you do not know who to ask. Telling them does not fix what makes it hard.*

**2. Name the real problem.** You learn that the signs and maps only make sense if you already know the building. What is the real problem?

- (careful design move) The signs and maps only work for kids who already know the school, so new kids feel lost.
  - *That is what is really going wrong, so your ideas can make the school clearer for new kids.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) New kids are too shy to ask questions, so the real problem is how they feel inside.
  - *Feeling shy is real, but the new kids said the signs were confusing. A clearer school would help them feel less shy.*
- (good idea, missing a piece) Teachers do not give enough directions, so the problem belongs to the teachers to fix.
  - *Teachers help a lot, but they cannot walk every new student around. Better signs would help every day.*

**3. Ideas (all three are workable).** You think of three ideas for helping new kids. Which one would you try first?

- (also good) Draw a colorful map with each hallway in its own color, so new kids can find their way.
  - *A map helps right away and anyone can use it. It only works if kids can read it and carry it around.*
- (also good) Pair each new student with a buddy for the first week who can walk with them to each place.
  - *A buddy gives kindness as well as directions. It needs kids who are ready to help and a way to match them.*
- (also good) Make a first-week passport with a stamp from each place, like the library and the gym.
  - *This makes learning the school feel like a game. It takes time to make, and kids have to remember to bring it.*

**4. Try a small test.** Before you change things for every new student, what is the best next step?

- (careful design move) Ask one new student to try your map for a day, then write down what is missing and fix it.
  - *A real test with a real new student shows what you missed, and fixing it makes the map better for everyone.*
- (also good) Ask a buddy to walk a new student through the passport for one day, and tell you what happened.
  - *You learn what works and what is confusing, and the buddy gets to practice being helpful.*
- (leaves people out or breaks a rule) Paint colored lines on the hallway floors during lunch, so that the map matches the building.
  - *Painting a school floor without permission could cause damage and trouble. Big changes need an adult to say yes first.*
- (ask the adult who can say yes) Ask the counselor, who knows when new kids arrive, if you can match buddies and try your map.
  - *Asking the counselor is a good move. They know the new students and can help your idea reach the right kids.*

**5. Private reflection.** Think about a time you were new somewhere. What helped you feel welcome, and what do you wish someone had done?
