# Money Matters: the reworked game, for your read (generated from the page)

Reworked 2026-11-01 as you approved on 2026-11-01 (Advance wave decision 2: real interest arithmetic, review document first). **Held: not committed or deployed until you have read it.** Fictional person and amounts; this is practice with ideas, not financial advice.

**How a run works:** each run draws 7 of the 14 events in their written order and shuffles the choices. Jordan starts with **$900 in checking, $400 in savings and a $500 card balance**. The goal is to reach **$800 in savings, keep at least $300 in checking, and bring the card balance to $400 or less**. A choice Jordan cannot afford shows greyed out ("not enough checking"). Every event has a choice that needs no money, so a run can never get stuck, and the card can never be paid below zero (the pay-down choices add up to $340, less than the $500 balance).

**What it teaches:** take-home pay after withholding, the save-20-percent rule, interest on a card (24 percent a year, about 2 percent a month), what a minimum payment really does, comparing a total price with a monthly price, switching fees, insurance deductibles, why an emergency fund is a month or more of costs, and what a better savings rate earns. SEL link: making a plan and weighing tradeoffs (NY SEL 3B direct, 1C supporting); Portrait of a Graduate: Reflective and Future Focused and Academically Prepared (supporting).

**What changed from the old game:** four fixed events with two choices each and made-up "Credit Score" points are gone. There is no longer a number that moves for no reason: every change in the three counters can be worked out from the event text.

## The 14 events

1. Jordan's part-time paycheck is $750 before taxes. About 20 percent is withheld ($150), so $600 arrives. A common rule saves 20 percent of take-home pay, which is $120. What does Jordan do?
   - Save $120 (20 percent) and keep $480 in checking.  (+480 Checking, +120 Savings)
   - Save half, $300, and keep $300 in checking.  (+300 Checking, +300 Savings)
   - Keep all $600 in checking.  (+600 Checking)

2. Jordan's car needs a $300 repair to keep getting to work. The card charges 24 percent a year. Paid off over 12 months, that adds about $40, so the repair would cost $340 in all.
   - Pay $300 from checking.  (-300 Checking)
   - Pay $300 from savings.  (-300 Savings)
   - Put it on the card and pay it off over 12 months ($340 in all).  (+340 Card Debt)
   - Get rides from a coworker this month and fix the car later.  (no change)

3. Jordan pays for three streaming services at $15 each, which is $45 a month, but only watches one. Over the next three months, what happens?
   - Cancel two services and keep $90 over three months.  (+90 Checking)
   - Cancel one service and keep $45 over three months.  (+45 Checking)
   - Keep all three for now.  (no change)

4. Groceries for the next four weeks: cooking at home costs about $60 a week ($240), and takeout costs about $140 a week ($560). A mix of two weeks each costs $400.
   - Cook at home all four weeks ($240).  (-240 Checking)
   - Cook two weeks and get takeout two weeks ($400).  (-400 Checking)
   - Get takeout all four weeks ($560).  (-560 Checking)
   - Eat what is already in the freezer and pantry this month.  (no change)

5. Jordan gets a $400 tax refund. The card balance charges about 2 percent a month, so every $100 paid off saves about $2 a month in interest.
   - Put all $400 in savings.  (+400 Savings)
   - Pay $100 on the card and save $300.  (-100 Card Debt, +300 Savings)
   - Keep $400 in checking for everyday costs.  (+400 Checking)

6. The card charges 24 percent a year, about 2 percent a month. On a $500 balance that is about $10 in interest each month. The minimum payment is $25, so only $15 of it lowers the balance.
   - Pay $100, which lowers the balance by $90 after the $10 interest.  (-100 Checking, -90 Card Debt)
   - Pay the $25 minimum, which lowers the balance by $15.  (-25 Checking, -15 Card Debt)
   - Skip the payment and let $10 of interest add to the balance.  (+10 Card Debt)

7. Experts often suggest saving one to three months of costs for emergencies. Jordan's costs are about $1,000 a month. Savings is still well under that. Move money from checking?
   - Move $200 from checking to savings.  (-200 Checking, +200 Savings)
   - Move $100 from checking to savings.  (-100 Checking, +100 Savings)
   - Leave it for now.  (no change)

8. Friends invite Jordan to a concert. The ticket is $60, food is $15, and the ride is $10, so the night costs $85. Putting it on the card at 24 percent a year and paying it off over 12 months costs about $96.
   - Go and pay $85 from checking.  (-85 Checking)
   - Go and put the night on the card ($96 in all).  (+96 Card Debt)
   - Suggest a free picnic at the park instead.  (no change)

9. Jordan needs a way to get around town. A new bike costs $300, a used bike costs $180, and a monthly bus pass costs $40, which is $240 over six months.
   - Buy the used bike ($180).  (-180 Checking)
   - Buy the new bike ($300).  (-300 Checking)
   - Buy a bus pass for six months ($240).  (-240 Checking)
   - Borrow a friend's bike for now.  (no change)

10. Jordan's savings account pays 0.5 percent a year. Another bank pays 4 percent. On $400 that is $2 a year compared with $16 a year, so $14 more. Switching takes about 20 minutes.
   - Switch banks and earn $14 more this year.  (+14 Savings)
   - Stay where it is.  (no change)

11. Jordan receives a $150 gift from a relative. Using it to pay down the card saves about $3 a month in interest, since 2 percent of $150 is $3.
   - Pay $150 toward the card.  (-150 Card Debt)
   - Put $150 in savings.  (+150 Savings)
   - Keep $150 in checking.  (+150 Checking)

12. Jordan compares car insurance for the next three months. Plan A costs $120 a month ($360) with a $500 deductible. Plan B costs $95 a month ($285) with a $1,000 deductible, so a crash would cost more out of pocket.
   - Choose Plan A ($360).  (-360 Checking)
   - Choose Plan B ($285).  (-285 Checking)
   - Stay on the old policy for now and compare again next month.  (no change)

13. Jordan's phone plan: the old plan costs $45 a month ($135 for three months). A new plan costs $30 a month ($90 for three months) and has a one-time $25 switching fee.
   - Switch and pay $115 now ($90 plus the $25 fee).  (-115 Checking)
   - Keep the old plan and pay $135.  (-135 Checking)
   - Look at plans again next month.  (no change)

14. A weekend shift pays $15 an hour for 8 hours, which is $120. About 10 percent is withheld ($12), so $108 arrives.
   - Take the shift and keep $108 in checking.  (+108 Checking)
   - Take the shift and save the $108.  (+108 Savings)
   - Skip the shift and rest.  (no change)

## The five endings (the game writes the dollar amounts in)

1. **All three goals** (savings at least $800, checking at least $300, card at most $400): "Jordan built savings, kept enough in checking, and brought the card balance down. ... There is more than one way to get there, and Jordan found one."
2. **Savings and checking, card still high:** "Jordan built a strong savings cushion and kept checking healthy. ... The card balance is still there, and paying it down is a sensible next step."
3. **Card down and checking healthy, savings short:** "Jordan brought the card balance down and kept checking healthy. ... Building savings is the next step, and a small cushion helps a lot."
4. **Checking running low:** "Jordan reached the end with checking running low. ... That is a real tradeoff, and some people choose it on purpose. A bigger buffer in checking makes surprises easier."
5. **Slow start:** "Jordan is off to a slow start, and that is normal for a first apartment. ... Try a different mix next time and see how it changes."

None of the endings calls a lower balance a failure.

## What a real player does (4,000 simulated runs, tested)

- No counter ever goes below zero, no run gets stuck, and all five endings are reached by a random player.
- A player who always picks a sensible option reaches the all-three-goals ending in about 45 percent of draws, and the savings-only ending in another 28 percent; a player who picks at random reaches the first ending in about 8 percent. A run with few income events (the paycheck, the refund, the gift, the weekend shift) is harder, which is realistic.
