# Fix-It Workshop (grades 3-5): the four-problem outline, for your approval

Written 2026-11-01. Outline only: no step text is written until you approve or edit each line. Concept approved 2026-11-01 (`NEW_GAMES_FOR_GAPS.md`, proposal 3). Each run plays one of four problems, the same way Choice Quest and Team Pick pick one of four stories.

**Links (draft, yours to sign off):** Portrait of a Graduate: Creative Innovator (direct); Critical Thinker, Effective Communicator and Global Citizen (supporting). NY SEL 3C Acting for school and community (direct); 2A Empathy and 3B Decision-making skills (supporting).

**How it plays (existing five-step story engine, no new code):** the same five steps as Choice Quest, used as a design cycle:

| Step (engine key) | In this game |
|---|---|
| Recognize | **Listen:** who has this problem, and how do you find out? |
| Impact | **Name the real problem:** what is actually going wrong, and for whom? |
| Consider | **Brainstorm:** three ideas, each with a different trade-off |
| Choose | **Try a small test:** how do you try the idea before changing everything? |
| Reflect | **Private reflection:** what would you change next time? (never saved or sent) |

**What is different from the other story games:** there is no single "responsible" answer at the idea step. All three ideas are workable; the feedback names what each is good at and what it costs ("quick and cheap, but it only helps on dry days"). The wrong answer at the other steps is the one that skips the people involved (you decided without asking, you blamed a group, you changed things without permission). That keeps the engine's answer types honest: *responsible* is the careful design move, *multiple defensible* is another good design move, *plausible but incomplete* is a good idea missing a piece, *unsafe* is a fix that breaks a rule or leaves someone out, *seek help* is asking the adult who can say yes.

**Writing rules a test will enforce (the same as Choice Quest and Team Pick):** three options in steps 1 to 3, four in the last decision step; balanced option lengths (within 1.4 times; the best answer the longest in at most 2 of 4 steps); feedback on each option that explains the effect; a private reflection; no dashes; fictional names; ideas that need only what a school could realistically try.

## The four problems

### 1. The lunch line is too long (school)
- **Who is affected:** students who get less time to eat, the lunch staff, the teachers who wait.
- **Listen:** count how long the line takes at different times; ask the lunch staff; ask a younger and an older student. (Wrong: ask only your own friends.)
- **Real problem:** the line is slow at the one register at the start of the period, not the food. (Wrong: students are too slow; the cafeteria is too small.)
- **Three ideas:** a second register for the first ten minutes; classes arrive in two groups, five minutes apart; a snack-pack "grab and go" cart. Each has a cost: money, schedule changes, or only some foods.
- **Small test:** try it for one week with one grade, count the line time again, and ask the students. (Wrong: change the schedule for the whole school on Monday.)
- **Seek help:** the principal or the lunch manager, who can approve a schedule or a register.

### 2. Nobody uses the quiet corner of the playground (recess, belonging)
- **Who is affected:** children who do not like crowded games, younger students, a group that stays inside.
- **Listen:** watch recess for two days and ask a few children what they do instead. (Wrong: guess that "kids are just lazy".)
- **Real problem:** the corner has no shade and nothing to do, so children skip it. (Wrong: children do not like quiet.)
- **Three ideas:** a drawing and chalk station; a reading nook with donated books; a calm-game box (cards, string, puzzles) kept by the teacher.
- **Small test:** open it for a week and count who uses it, then ask them what to change.
- **Seek help:** a recess aide who can watch the corner, or the teacher who can store the box.

### 3. The lost-and-found is full and nobody checks it (school, caring for belongings)
- **Who is affected:** children who lose coats and lunch boxes, families who pay for replacements, the custodian.
- **Listen:** look at what is in the box, ask a few classes what they have lost, ask the custodian how often it empties.
- **Real problem:** nobody knows what is in it. (Wrong: children are careless.)
- **Three ideas:** a photo board in the hallway; a "found it" table at dismissal every Friday; a name-label day with a sticker for each child.
- **Small test:** try the photo board for two weeks and count how many items are claimed.
- **Seek help:** the office, who can approve a spot on the wall, or a parent volunteer.

### 4. New students find the building confusing (welcome, inclusion)
- **Who is affected:** new students, children who show them around, teachers who answer the same questions.
- **Listen:** ask two students who joined this year what they found hard, and what helped. (Wrong: ask only the students who have been here the longest.)
- **Real problem:** the signs and the map assume you already know the building. (Wrong: new students should just ask.)
- **Three ideas:** a student-drawn map with colour-coded halls; a buddy for the first week; a "first-week passport" with a stamp from each place.
- **Small test:** one new student tries the map for a day and says what is missing.
- **Seek help:** the counselor, who knows when new students arrive and can match buddies.

## Spread checks

- **Each problem leaves the idea step open:** three workable ideas, three different strengths (fast, kind, lasting).
- **The best answer is sometimes the shortest option**, so length does not give it away.
- **The unsafe option is believable**, such as "put up signs without asking" or "tell the class what to do", not a cartoon.
- **Reading level:** about grades 3 to 5 on the same readability estimate used for the other Explore games.
