# Community Voice (grades 9-12): four situations, for your approval

Written 2026-11-01. Outline only: no step text is written until you approve or edit each line. Concept approved 2026-11-01 (`NEW_GAMES_FOR_GAPS.md`, proposal 6). Each run would play one of four stories.

**Links (draft, yours to sign off):** NY SEL 3C Acting for school and community well-being (direct), 2C Communicating across differences (supporting), 3B Decision-making (supporting). Portrait of a Graduate: Global Citizen (direct), Effective Communicator (direct), Critical Thinker (supporting, because the game asks for checking facts). After this, Effective Communicator and 3C each have two direct games.

**What the game is:** you want the school or town to change one small thing. The game walks through how to do it well: listen to who is affected, check the facts, plan a respectful ask, present it to the person who decides, and handle a "no." Topics are everyday and non-partisan, with no party, candidate or real organization.

**How each story plays (existing five-step story engine, no new code):** the five steps become a civic cycle:

| Step (engine key) | In this game |
|---|---|
| Recognize | **Listen:** who is affected, and how do you find out? |
| Impact | **Check the facts:** what do you actually know, and what are you assuming? |
| Consider | **Plan the ask:** how will you present it? |
| Choose | **Speak up:** what do you do when you meet the decision maker, and when the answer is "no"? |
| Reflect | **Private reflection** (never saved or sent) |

Steps 1 to 3 give three options (one careful, two believable but incomplete); step 4 gives four (careful, also good, unsafe, ask a trusted adult). Feedback on every option explains the effect. The writing rules are the ones a test enforces for the other story games: balanced option lengths (within 1.4 times; the careful answer the longest in at most 2 of the 4 judged steps), no dashes, fictional names, a believable "unsafe" option. In this game "unsafe" means a move that makes the cause harder to win or that treats people badly: a flood of complaints, going around the decision maker, or spreading a complaint online.

## The four stories

### 1. A later activity bus (decision maker: the school's transportation office)
**Situation:** the last bus leaves at 3:15, so students in clubs and sports practice cannot get home. You want an activity bus at 5:00. The transportation office says it is a cost question.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Ask different students, including some who are not your friends, who would use a later bus and why | "Ask only your own club, since you know them best"; "Assume that most students want it" |
| Impact | Find out how many students stay late, and what a bus costs, so that you know what you are asking for | "Use a rough guess like 'tons of kids'"; "Skip the cost, because that is the school's job" |
| Consider | Prepare a short, specific ask with numbers and one clear request, such as a trial of two days a week | "Bring a long list of complaints about transportation"; "Ask for a full daily bus right away" |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** present the ask calmly, listen to the cost concern, and ask what would make a trial possible. **Also good:** offer to count riders for two weeks so the office can see the need. **Unsafe:** post a complaint about the office online and tell classmates to flood their inbox. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask a teacher or advisor to help you find the right person and process. | |
| Reflect | "What would you want someone to do if they disagreed with a decision you made?" | |

### 2. A quieter study space (decision maker: the librarian and the principal)
**Situation:** the library is loud after school, and students who need quiet cannot study. You would like a quiet room or a quiet hour. The librarian says space is limited, and the principal asks how many students would use it.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Ask students who study in the library, and students who go elsewhere, what they need | "Ask the students who are making the noise to stop"; "Assume that everyone agrees with you" |
| Impact | Check how the room is used now, and when it is loudest, so that you can propose a real time or place | "Say that the library is always too loud"; "Count only the people who have complained" |
| Consider | Suggest a small trial, such as a quiet table or a quiet hour, and say how you would measure whether it works | "Ask the library to ban talking"; "Ask for a separate room, since that would be the best" |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** present the trial, thank the librarian for the limits, and ask what would work with the space. **Also good:** offer to help set up signs and a sign-up sheet for the trial. **Unsafe:** put up your own "quiet only" signs without asking. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask the librarian or a teacher for the right way to propose it. | |
| Reflect | "How do you decide whose needs come first when two groups want different things?" | |

### 3. More fresh options in the cafeteria (decision maker: the food service director)
**Situation:** the cafeteria offers few fresh fruit and vegetable choices, and many students skip them. You want more. The food service director says the budget and the rules limit what can be served.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Survey a wide group of students, not only your friends, about what they would actually eat | "Ask only the students who already eat fresh food"; "Assume that students want what you want" |
| Impact | Find out what the budget and the rules allow, so that your ask is something the director can actually say yes to | "Assume that the director just does not care"; "Ignore the rules, since they can change" |
| Consider | Suggest one small, specific change, such as a fresh option two days a week, with a way to see whether students choose it | "Ask for a full menu change"; "Ask for a petition with lots of names and no plan" |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** present your survey and the small change, and ask what would make it possible. **Also good:** offer to run a taste test day to show student interest. **Unsafe:** pressure the director with a petition of names that students did not really agree to. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask a teacher or counselor to introduce you to the right person. | |
| Reflect | "When have you had to accept a limit and still work for a change?" | |

### 4. A crosswalk near the school (decision maker: the town's traffic committee)
**Situation:** students cross a busy road to reach the school, and it feels unsafe. You want a marked crosswalk. The town's traffic committee asks for data and a written request, and meets once a month.

| Step | Careful option | Believable but incomplete |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize | Talk to students, parents and neighbors who use the crossing, and note what they say | "Rely on your own feeling that it is dangerous"; "Ask only people who already agree with you" |
| Impact | Gather facts, such as when students cross and how many, and look at what the committee asks for | "Say that it is obviously dangerous and skip the facts"; "Count on stories about accidents, even if you are not sure they are true" |
| Consider | Write a short request with the data and a clear ask, and plan to attend the next meeting | "Send an angry email"; "Wait for someone else to bring it up" |
| Choose (4) | **Careful:** attend the meeting, present the facts calmly, and ask what the committee needs next. **Also good:** offer to count crossings for a week and share the results. **Unsafe:** block the road or film drivers to make a point. **Ask a trusted adult:** ask a teacher, parent or the school's safety officer to help with the request. | |
| Reflect | "What would you do next if the committee said not yet?" | |

## Spread checks

- **Four different decision makers and four different kinds of pushback:** cost, space, rules and process. The careful move changes with each, and "ask an adult" is the fourth option, never the first.
- **Each story teaches a different civic skill:** a small trial, checking use, working inside limits, and data for a formal request.
- **The game links to Critical Read:** students are asked to check facts and avoid asking only their friends, the same skills the reading games practise.
- **No story puts the student alone on the hook:** each offers a trusted-adult option, and nothing in it tells a student to defy anyone. The unsafe options are the ones that hurt the cause.
- **Non-partisan:** no party, candidate, real organization or contested issue appears.
- **Reading level:** about grade 8 to 12, plain and concrete.
